# R7: Building large business software as a solo founder with AI coding agents; build vs buy vs extend

Researcher R7 of 7. Angle: what the evidence says about AI coding agents at scale, which architecture and process choices help or hurt them, what small teams have actually built, and the build / extend / rewrite decision for this owner.

Method note: the shared web-search budget for this turn ran out partway through. After that I fetched only primary URLs I already knew. Items I could not verify are marked "not verified" or "not found". Nothing here is legal advice. The licence conclusions need a lawyer before any money is committed.

## 2. What one founder with agents can realistically build and maintain

### 2.1 What the evidence says, without the hype

1. **Writing code is cheap now. Owning it is not.** METR measured experienced developers on mature, high-standard repos: 19% slower in early 2025 [1], and somewhere between slightly slower and clearly faster in late 2025, with wide intervals and acknowledged selection bias [2]. DORA finds AI raises throughput but keeps lowering stability [5][6], and it calls AI an amplifier of whatever testing, feedback and architecture already exist [6]. Taken together: agents speed up typing, but the binding constraints are verification, review, domain correctness and operations. In a one-person company, all of those land on the founder.
2. **Agent-written code drifts toward duplication and away from refactoring** [3]. It also arrives with misplaced confidence, both in security [13] and in self-assessed speed [1]. A large codebase written mostly by agents needs deliberate de-duplication and refactoring. The tools will not do it unprompted.
3. **Benchmarks overstate agent ability on long, multi-file business changes.** SWE-bench Verified is contaminated [8]. On multi-file tasks averaging about 107 changed lines, frontier models solved under 25% at launch [7]. Cross-module ERP features (for example, posting a stock movement that also creates journal entries, taxes and an e-CF) are exactly that kind of task.
4. **The strongest "agents built a huge system" case needed a team and a heavy harness.** OpenAI's experiment reached about 1M LOC in 5 months, but with 3–7 engineers each driving 10–50 agents, about 1B tokens/day, sub-minute builds, deep package decomposition and automated review. The product was an internal beta, not a regulated, multi-country accounting system [9]. This is the upper bound, and it was not one person.
5. **ERP breadth has historically taken an organisation.** Odoo had 100+ staff five years after TinyERP [15] and 2,200+ today. Frappe has about 83 people [19]. The small teams that won sold narrow, deep products: Plausible (two founders) [21], 37signals ONCE [23], Levels' single-file PHP products [29]. Basecamp ran about 12 programmers on one monolith [24].

**What is realistic.** One founder with agents can build and keep correct **one deep, narrow product**: a DR e-CF compliance engine or connector, or a vertical CRM plus invoicing. They can also **compose** a breadth product out of a framework someone else maintains.

What is not realistic is a hand-built ERP (GL, inventory, purchasing, payroll, reports, print, custom fields, workflows, many countries) kept to a strict quality bar by one person. That is not a code-volume problem; agents can produce the volume. The problem is that every line must be understood, verified, migrated and supported when DGII or another regulator changes the rules. The repo already has 55k production lines plus 72k test lines after two weeks. That shows the generation rate is not the constraint, and that the maintenance surface is growing faster than one person can review it (DORA's instability finding [5][6]).

### 2.2 Choices that make agents effective

What this repo already does lines up with the evidence:

- **A pass/fail check the agent can run**: types, tests, linters as a gate [11]. The repo's strict basedpyright/mypy, zero suppressions, 100% branch coverage and `poe check` are exactly this.
- **Enforced boundaries in a modular monolith**: import-linter layering, one schema per module. OpenAI's harness used the same idea (strict interfaces, deep decomposition) [9]. DORA ties AI gains to loosely coupled architecture [6]. Fowler and DHH say a monolith with real internal boundaries is the right starting shape for a small team [24][27].
- **Specs, ADRs and a short AGENTS.md as the map**, with rules encoded once in docs, skills or lint instead of patched per incident [9][11]. A feature list plus a progress file plus git as memory across sessions [10].
- **Boring technology** [28]: Postgres, Python, SQLAlchemy Core. Agents also know boring stacks best.

What to add or tighten:

- **Single-writer edits with a clean-context reviewer.** Cognition found parallel writer swarms produce conflicting implicit decisions [12]. The repo was built "in ~2 weeks with parallel AI agents". Keep parallelism for independent modules in separate worktrees and for read-only research or review, never for concurrent writes to shared chassis code.
- **A fast inner loop.** OpenAI capped builds at about a minute [9]. If `poe check`, with mutation testing and 72k test lines, takes many minutes, agents will batch bigger changes and verify less often. Split it into a fast per-module gate and a slower full gate.
- **Measure duplication and churn** per module (any clone detector) [3], and schedule refactoring and garbage-collection passes like OpenAI's daily process [9].
- **Separate test intent from implementation.** When the same agent writes both the test and the code, it can verify its own misunderstanding: 100% coverage proves the code was executed, not that it is correct. For fiscal rules, the tests should come from DGII's official samples and TesteCF results, not from the agent's reading of the spec. This is the defence against the overconfidence in [13].

### 2.3 Choices that make agents dangerous

- **Code volume beyond review capacity.** Thousands of lines a day with no one who can review them gives you DORA's instability pattern [5][6] and GitClear's duplication pattern [3].
- **A hand-built chassis as a core asset.** Every chassis line is context the agents must load and code the founder must own. The 16.5k-line chassis (30% of production code) re-creates what a framework would supply: tenancy, jobs, outbox, rate limits, errors. It costs innovation tokens [28] that buy no customer-visible difference.
- **Instruction bloat.** Long CLAUDE.md/AGENTS.md files and handoff docs get ignored [11]. The fix is to move rules into hooks and checks, not into more prose.
- **Using benchmark-level trust for regulated logic** [7][8][13]. E-CF signing, numbering and tax totals need certification evidence (TesteCF), not agent self-reports.

## 3. Build vs extend vs rewrite for this owner

The starting point: about 55k production lines (about 16.5k chassis, the rest invoicing, fiscal, signer and the DR e-CF country pack) and about 72k test lines. It was built in about two weeks, is not in production, has no UI, and is aimed at a CRM with ERP features.

### Option A: build CRM and ERP breadth on the current chassis

- **What is missing**: a metadata layer (custom fields, views, record rules), a UI, a report and print engine, runtime extensibility, and a cross-module read path. The earlier jury already said so (`docs/reviews/2026-10-20-stack-jury-synthesis.md`, §4). Odoo and Frappe each represent many years and dozens to thousands of people's work on exactly those layers [15][17][19].
- **Agent economics**: an ERP is many long, cross-module changes, which is the task class where agents are weakest [7]. The amount to verify grows with breadth, not with typing speed [1][2][6].
- **Verdict**: technically possible, but it does not fit one person. It is Netscape-style time lost to building plumbing [26], except here the plumbing is the ERP framework itself.

### Option B: rewrite (another stack, or a fresh start)

- **Joel's argument mostly does not apply** [26]. The code has no years of field-earned bug fixes and no customers to migrate. In DHH's terms, nobody is forced off anything [25]. The direct rewrite cost is small, on the order of the original two weeks of agent time.
- **The real costs are elsewhere**:
  - Delay against the e-CF deadline window.
  - Re-verification: TesteCF/DGII certification has to be redone for any new e-CF implementation.
  - Lost domain knowledge, unless the specs, fixtures and tests come along.
- **What a rewrite would not fix**: it does not solve "one person cannot own ERP breadth". Rewriting the same custom chassis in TypeScript or Go changes the language, not the problem. The evidence favours boring, well-known stacks [28], and Python with Postgres already is one.
- **What has value whatever the stack**: the e-CF domain knowledge (XML builders for every e-CF type, DGII client behaviour, the state machine, numbering, receiver and signer custody), the spec and ADRs, and the test fixtures. Port these; do not rewrite them from memory.
- **Verdict**: a full rewrite is unjustified. A partial "rewrite" is justified only as **replacing the chassis with a framework** where the product needs breadth (see C/D). That is Fowler-style boundary evolution [27], not a big-bang rewrite.

### Option C: extend an open-source ERP or CRM

| Base | Licence consequences for a SaaS seller (needs legal review) | Fit |
|---|---|---|
| **Odoo Community** | LGPLv3 core [14]; your own modules may be proprietary. Enterprise is subscription-only and cannot be redistributed [14], so you cannot build a product on Enterprise features. Odoo S.A. controls direction and also sells SaaS. | Full ERP breadth. The DR e-CF module market already exists (the jury noted modules at about US$80–295), so you compete on the module, not on the ERP. |
| **ERPNext** | GPL-3 [16]. Running it as SaaS does not trigger GPL source obligations, because GPL is triggered by distribution, not network use. Distributing apps that import ERPNext to self-hosters likely puts them under GPL. | Full ERP breadth plus Frappe Cloud hosting. A DR localization app is a natural product. |
| **Frappe Framework alone** | MIT [17]: you can build a proprietary SaaS on it. | Gives the metadata, UI, permissions, REST, report builder and MCP server [17] that this chassis lacks. You write the CRM/ERP apps, or reuse ERPNext modules under GPL. |
| **Twenty** | AGPLv3, with proprietary apps allowed if they don't modify the core; enterprise files are commercial [20]. A modified Twenty served as SaaS must publish its source. | CRM only (no GL, inventory or e-CF). TypeScript stack, so a different language from the engine. |

The licence lesson from Plausible and Cal.com [21][22]: if you open-source your own engine, AGPL protects you against hosted clones, and open-core boundaries get redrawn and forked over time. Being open source is not the moat; certification, support and distribution are.

### Option D: hybrid (my recommendation for analysis)

1. **Keep the current repo as a headless e-CF compliance engine.** Freeze chassis growth and stop adding generic platform features. Sell it first to software vendors, integrators and accountants through an API, with connectors for Odoo, ERPNext and others. This is the narrow, deep product a solo founder can own (§2.1). It also matches the jury's go-to-market.
2. **Take CRM and ERP breadth from a metadata framework** instead of hand-building it. Pick Odoo CE or Frappe (MIT framework, GPL ERPNext) through the jury's 5-day experiment. Integrate with the engine over its API, not by forking the engine into the framework (the jury's isolation point on key custody and tenancy).
3. **The cost of D** is integration work and living with the framework's conventions and upgrades. That is far cheaper than building a metadata layer, UI, reporting and accounting, and framework upgrades are work that agents handle well (fan-out migrations [11]).

**Cost of a rewrite, summarised**: the direct cost is low (weeks of agent time, no migration). The indirect cost is high: delay in a deadline-driven market, re-certification, and an unchanged scope problem. The one rewrite worth doing is replacing the hand-built chassis with a framework **for the breadth product only**. The engine keeps its chassis because e-CF signing, archiving and key custody justify it.

## 4. Verdict

- **The current code is not on the wrong path as a fiscal engine, but it is the wrong base for CRM and ERP breadth.** The strict gate, typed modular monolith, ADRs and specs are what the evidence says makes agents effective [6][9][11][27][28]. A hand-built chassis with no metadata, UI or report layer cannot become an Odoo-class product owned by one person, because verification and ownership, not code generation, are the binding constraints [1][2][5][6].
- **Do not rewrite.** There are no customers or field-earned fixes at stake, so Joel's specific warning is weak here [25][26]. But a rewrite costs time and re-certification without solving the breadth problem. Port and keep the e-CF domain logic, specs and fixtures, and freeze chassis growth.
- **Extend, don't build, for breadth.** Use Odoo CE (LGPL; proprietary modules allowed, Enterprise off-limits) or Frappe (MIT framework, GPL ERPNext) for CRM, accounting and inventory, decided by the 5-day experiment and a lawyer's licence review. Connect it to the engine over an API. Use Twenty (AGPL) only if the product becomes CRM-first [14][16][17][20].
- **Run agents as single writers behind fast, deterministic gates.** Use a clean-context reviewer, test cases from DGII's official samples and TesteCF rather than agent-authored expectations, and duplication and churn tracking. Avoid parallel writers on shared code and long prose instruction files [3][10][11][12][13].
- **Sequence like the small teams that won.** Ship one narrow, certified product (the e-CF engine or connector sold to vendors, integrators and accountants), get revenue, and add breadth only where paying customers pull for it. Small teams succeed with narrow products and boring stacks; ERP breadth has historically taken an organisation [15][19][21][23][24][29].
